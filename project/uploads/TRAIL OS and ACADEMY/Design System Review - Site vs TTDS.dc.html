Design system review

The site and the design system have never met

Not a conformance problem so much as a wiring one. The site does not import a single design-system token — it hardcodes hex values in Tailwind brackets, 1,580 of them in the page files alone. Most land on the right brand colours by hand, which is why nothing looks obviously wrong. It also means every near-miss is invisible until you go looking, and nothing the design system changes can ever reach the site.

01 · The wiring

Two token sets that do not know about each other

The site defines its own brand variables in its stylesheet — and gets the four brand colours and four neutrals exactly right. Then almost nothing uses them. Components write the hex value inline instead, so the variables sit there being correct and unread.

Fraunces spread because there was no token saying otherwise. Fix the wiring and the rest become one-line changes; leave it and this review is due again in six months.
02 · Colour

The brand colours are right. The shades around them are improvised.

Trail Green, Deep Teal, Sky Blue, Sun Amber and all four neutrals match the system exactly wherever they appear. The problem is everything between them — tints and shades invented at the point of use, several of them a hair away from a token that already exists.

Kept, because it makes the H1 and H2 headings genuinely stand out in a way Poppins does not at display sizes. The system becomes three faces plus the accent, with the roles written down: Fraunces for H1 and H2 only, Poppins for H3, H4, buttons and labels, Open Sans for body and UI, Caveat once per page at most.

The part that matters is the boundary. Fraunces earns its place at display sizes and loses it below them — without the H1/H2 limit written into the system it will creep down into H3 and subheads, and the contrast that makes it work now disappears.
04 · Shape and depth

Corners are tighter and shadows are greyer than the system says

Both of these read as slightly more corporate than intended. Neither is wrong on its own; together they cost the site some of the softness the brand is going for.

05 · The amber rule

One amber call to action per screen, and we were running four

The brand book is unusually firm here: never more than one Sun Amber CTA per screen, never decorative. It was the strongest rule in the system and the most consistently broken one — now settled, and sent to the site.

On a typical program page you will find amber in four roles at once
·{{ a.text }}
Decided: the nav button gives up amber, the in-page CTA keeps it. The nav button is identical on every page, so it cannot be the next step anywhere — it is a fallback. The real next step differs per page, and that is what amber should track. On the teal bar it becomes solid white with teal text, which is higher contrast than the amber was; solid green if the bar ever goes light on scroll.
The rule, in three partsOne amber button per screen — the next step on that page. Amber text on dark surfaces only, using amber-300, which keeps the eyebrows on the charcoal heroes where they do real hierarchy work and keeps amber text off light backgrounds where it fails contrast anyway. Amber tint fills allowed but never beside the amber button — "MOST POPULAR" on a program card is fine; the same badge next to the page's CTA is not.

The one place needing real rework was the full-bleed amber band at the foot of Guided Trail — an amber section containing an amber button. The band goes deep green and the button carries the amber.
Approved, and the ramps are the reason it is safe: because every stop comes from a defined ramp, a gradient is on-palette by construction rather than by taste. The brand book's blanket "no gradients" line goes.

What replaces it is three named recipes — green, teal, and deep — all at 135 degrees, and nothing else. The drift so far came from hand-mixing stops page by page, not from gradients as an idea. One rule worth keeping: never behind body copy, only behind display type and generous whitespace.

07 · What to do

In this order, and none of it this week

Nothing here blocks launch. The site looks consistent because the hardcoded values are mostly right — the risk is drift over the next year, not appearance next Tuesday.
