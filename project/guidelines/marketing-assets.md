# Marketing assets: LinkedIn and Substack

Use this guide for Transition Trails organization posts, bylined articles, and companion visuals. It supplements the [TTDS foundations](../README.md); it does not change the brand palette, type rules, [wordmark](brand-wordmark.html), or the program and product design branches.

## Choose the audience and the message

| Layout | Use it for | Start with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Learners | Getting started, building confidence through practice, or showing skills in a portfolio | A trail illustration for an invitation. A redacted Build With Me screenshot or real portfolio artifact for a concrete example. |
| Donors | Invitations to support access, stewardship, or an update on progress | A bridge or growth illustration for a general message. A documented project artifact, an approved photo, or a chart of verified results for an appeal tied to impact. |
| Nonprofit Partners | Starting a conversation about a workflow, scoping an improvement, or inviting a partner to collaborate | Clearer Path (trail with wooden waymarkers) for the invitation. Use the Project Spotlight layout and approved evidence for a named client or case study. |
| Project Spotlight | Showing what a learner or team actually did | An approved photo, redacted screenshot, or workflow diagram grounded in the project. State the problem, what changed, and why it mattered. |

Use organization voice for Transition Trails announcements. For an article or post signed by a founder, follow the relevant [founder voice guide](founder-voices.md). Keep the headline specific to the message; the audience label and footer do not carry the story.

## Use illustration for a metaphor, not for evidence

- Use trail illustrations for concepts such as starting out, access, or progress. Prefer paths, landscapes, and objects. Skip AI-generated hands.
- Do not present a generated scene as a photo of a learner, participant, partner, or project. If an illustration could be mistaken for documentary imagery, label it "AI-generated illustration" in the caption or accompanying text. Write alt text that describes the image; do not rely on alt text alone for disclosure.
- Confirm permission before using a real person's image or a named partner's material. Redact personal and client data from screenshots. Source numbers and outcomes before turning them into graphics. If evidence is unavailable, flag it rather than simulating it.

## Use the campaign sign-off in the footer

On these marketing templates, use the exact line: **Real Experience. Real Impact. On the Trail.** Keep it secondary to the headline and readable at feed or cover size. It is a marketing sign-off, not a replacement for the existing [wordmark specimen](brand-wordmark.html).

## Reuse the Canva layouts

- **LinkedIn:** duplicate a page from *Transition Trails - LinkedIn Post Templates* (square). Preserve the headline, image area, and footer as separate editable elements; keep the call to action relevant to the post.
- **Substack:** duplicate a page from *Transition Trails - Substack Cover Templates* (landscape). Check the article and email previews separately. The cover is not the publication's email masthead.
- Both sets have Learners, Donors, Nonprofit Partners, and Project Spotlight layouts. Find them by title in the Transition Trails Canva workspace. Use the team-approved version. Do not include private Canva edit links in this public repository.
- Reuse the same illustration across the article and its LinkedIn companion when the message is the same. Otherwise choose an image for the new angle, not simply the next one in rotation. Use TTDS color and type guidance for new versions.

## Handoff for Claude

For each visual, provide one line in Writing Studio:

`Audience | message | Canva layout | image ID or evidence needed | destination and placement | draft alt text`

Use one cover by default. Add a second image only if it explains something the cover does not. Never mark an asset ready based on a brief or a Canva link alone: the exported image must be attached and checked in the destination draft.

Before handoff, confirm the crop, contrast, legibility, alt text, required disclosures, and any permissions. Leave scheduling and publishing to the designated reviewer.
