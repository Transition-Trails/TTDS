Nonprofit Trail Kit series

A plan for what we build, in what order, and — the part that matters more — what we decline to build. One kit at a time, each one earning the next.

The thesis
A nonprofit buys a Trail Kit because they have one specific thing to switch on, no admin to ask, and no budget for a consultant. The kit earns its price because it says why it matters to your mission, not just which buttons to press — and because it tells them how to undo it.
Who is actually reading it
Usually one person, part-time on Salesforce and full-time on something else. Development director, operations manager, or the person who happened to be nearest the laptop when the org was set up. They are capable and short on time, not junior.
They are not learning Salesforce as a career move. They want this one thing working so they can go back to their actual job.
What this series is not
Not a certification path, not a curriculum, and not a substitute for our programs. A kit is a single job done well.
It is also not lead-generation dressed as a product. If a kit only makes sense as a funnel into a program, it is not a kit — it is an ad, and people can tell.

The honest test
If free documentation already explains it well and nothing goes wrong in practice, there is no kit here. We are paid for the failure modes, the judgment calls, and the permission to stop — not for restating a help article with nicer type.
So ask it concretely: what goes wrong for someone following the official article? If you cannot name three specific failures from having hit them yourself, there is no kit yet — there is a topic you have not done enough times.
This is also the test that keeps the series honest as it grows. The pressure to fill a monthly slot is exactly when a thin kit gets made.

Nonprofit kit series · Internal planningSequencing & production

Order, bundle, and what each kit costs to make

Why this order
Board report first. It scores highest, it is the most visible win, and it is the one a buyer shows someone else — which is how the second kit sells itself.
Then duplicates. Clean data makes every later kit work better, and a board report built on duplicates is the moment people discover they have them.
Hold the spreadsheet import until later — it scores 2 on reversibility, and a kit that can lose someone's donor list is a reputational risk we should take deliberately, not third.
Bundling
Hold the single-kit list price as it stands in the record — low enough to be an impulse for an organization, high enough to signal it is not a lead magnet. Do not discount below it.
Bundle by series, not by count — a bundle goes on sale when the series it belongs to is complete, which is three kits for two of them and four for Numbers you can defend. A partial bundle invites the question of what else is coming.
Bundle price is your call; the shape I would suggest is a clear per-kit saving rather than a round number, so it reads as arithmetic rather than a sale.

The step from kit to membership
This is not a price ladder, and we should stop drawing it as one. A kit and an entry membership are two orders of magnitude apart — dozens of kits to equal one year of Compass. No amount of kit-buying walks a buyer up to it.
What changes is who does the work. A kit buyer does it themselves; a Compass member has decided they would rather not.
So the kit's job is proof, not progression. A buyer who finishes one and thinks "I would pay these people to help" is the conversion.
Seats, and why they are generous
Compass members are organizations, not individuals. Three seats — you and two colleagues — because one person learning alone is how knowledge leaves when they do.
Educating people is the mission. Seat limits size the service, they do not ration it — every conversation about them should sound that way.
A verified nonprofit's kit license is already organization-wide, so the PDFs stay shareable internally. Seats cover what needs a person: office hours, and the reviews.
Confirmed in the program design
The kit library is a benefit at every Compass tier, already settled — so the series is a membership asset, not just a product line.
Individual learners belong on Explorer's Trail — its own tiers, not Compass. Learner-edition kits point there.
