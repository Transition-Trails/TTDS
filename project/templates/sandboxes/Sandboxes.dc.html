Sandboxes

A copy of your org for building and testing. Developer Edition orgs do not get one — so if you are working from a free practice org, read this to understand what your workplace has, not to follow along.

Logging in afterwards
Your username gains the sandbox name. If you are you@example.com in production, you are you@example.com.uat in a sandbox named UAT. Your email address does not change — your login does.
You log in at a different address. Sandboxes live at test.salesforce.com, not the usual one. The normal login page will simply reject you.
Refreshing destroys it
A refresh does not update your sandbox. It replaces it with a new copy of production. Everything you built there and did not deploy is gone, with no undo and no export.
Tell anyone else using it before you refresh. This is how people lose a week of someone else's work without ever knowing.
Getting the change into production
Change sets are the click path. Build an outbound set in the sandbox, upload it, then validate the inbound set in production before deploying.
Data never travels. A change set moves configuration only. Records you created for testing stay behind, which is usually what you want and occasionally a surprise.
Not everything is eligible. Some settings cannot go in a change set at all and have to be redone by hand. Find out which before you promise a date.
Reporting is the awkward one
A report is only as testable as the data underneath it. A Developer sandbox has no records, so every report returns nothing and you cannot tell a correct one from a broken one. A Partial Copy is worse than empty — filters match a handful of rows, totals look plausible and are wrong, and you carry that false confidence into production.
So build reports in production. They read data and do not change it, and one saved to Private is visible to nobody until you share it. Get it right against real numbers, then move the folder — this is the one case where the careful-looking route is the less reliable one.
