Trail Kit 02 · Nonprofit editionTab 1 · Why

Four surfaces, one job

They are usually taught as four separate features. They are really one system for putting an answer in front of a person, and each one reaches someone the others miss.

Report
The answer itself, live every time it opens. Powerful, and invisible until someone goes looking for it.
List view
The working surface. People do their day here, so a good one changes behavior more than any dashboard.
Dashboard
Several answers at a glance, for the weekly conversation. It summarizes; it is not where work happens.
Home page
The only surface people see without choosing to. This is why it is the adoption lever, and why it is usually left as the default.
Why people export anyway
Almost never because the builder is hard. Because they could not find a starting point with their fields in it, the numbers did not match what they expected, or the thing they needed was three clicks off their daily path. All three are fixable, and none of them are a training problem.
A number your board trusts is worth more than a system nobody questions. This is the work that makes Salesforce visible to the people who fund it. A dashboard is the most satisfying thing to build and the least likely to be used daily. Build it for the board meeting, but do not mistake it for the win. Ask someone what they keep in a spreadsheet. That spreadsheet is your specification, and they will hand it to you gladly.

Find the question first

Nobody wants a report. They want to stop wondering something — usually a director, a board member, or a funder. Write down the wondering, in their words, before you open Salesforce.

1
Whose question is it?
One named person, not "the board". If you cannot name who will look at this on a Tuesday, you are building it for a meeting rather than for work.
2
What will they do differently?
A number that changes no decision is decoration. Ask what action follows from a high answer and a low one.
3
How often do they need it?
Daily means a list view. Weekly means a dashboard. Quarterly means a report they can find. The answer decides what you build.
4
What number are they expecting?
Ask before you build. If your result differs, you have found either a data problem or a misunderstanding — and you want to find it now, not in the meeting.
Access you need
Permission to create reports and dashboards, a folder to save into, and — for the home page step — permission to edit Lightning pages. If one person holds all the admin access here, that is a separate risk worth raising.
Practicing first? Three CSV files came with your purchase. Load them rather than experimenting in your live org.
Ask for their spreadsheet. The columns they kept are the fields that matter, and the ones they stopped filling in tell you what never mattered. Never build the second thing they ask for. Build the first, put it in front of them, and let their reaction rewrite the rest of the list.

Step 1 is a report type. Always.

Before you open the builder, build the thing the builder draws from. This step decides whether reporting in your org is good or permanently frustrating.

Do not use standard report types
Salesforce ships one per object and they look like a shortcut. You cannot control which fields they expose — custom fields on related objects are frequently unreachable, and you cannot add them.
Someone builds a report, cannot find the field they need, concludes Salesforce cannot do it, and exports. Every custom report type you build removes one of those moments permanently.
There is no switch to hide them. Salesforce gives no supported way to remove standard types from the picker — so you crowd them out: build a custom type for every question people ask, and file them in categories named for teams.
Setup, then Report Types. Primary object is the thing being counted. Read the join choice aloud — must have excludes rows, may or may not have keeps them.
Your report type, with its join choices
Open the field layout and add the custom fields. A field missing from the report type does not exist as far as your users are concerned. Add more than you need — unused columns cost nothing.
The field layout, with your custom fields added
Name it as the question people ask, and fill in the description — both appear in the picker when someone clicks New Report. Leave it In Development and only admins can see it. This is the most common reason a colleague cannot find what you just built. Build the report type wider than today's question. The second request always arrives, and adding a field later means editing the layout again.

Steps 3–4: the answer, and where work happens

Paste your own screenshots in the boxes as you go.

Filter, group, summarize. Then compare with the number they gave you in Tab 2. A mismatch is information, not failure — go and find out which of you is right.
Your report, with its total
Same filters, fewer columns. A list view is where someone acts on the answer rather than reading it, so include only what they need to decide and click.
Your list view
Name it as the question. "Grants closing this quarter" beats "Opportunity Report 4" and needs no explaining. Save into a folder people can actually see. A report in your private folder does not exist to anyone else. Fewer columns than feels right. Every extra one is a decision you are asking someone to make daily.

Steps 5–6: the glance, and the front door

Build it for a specific meeting. If you cannot name the conversation it supports, you are decorating. Set the running user deliberately — it decides whose data everyone sees.
Your dashboard
Edit the Lightning home page, add the dashboard or list view component, and assign it to the right app and profile. This is the step people skip, and it is the step that decides whether any of the rest gets used.
The home page as they will see it
Assign the home page to a profile, not to everyone, until one person has used it for a week. A bad home page is the most visible mistake you can make in an org. Log in as them if you can, or sit beside them. What you see from an admin login is never what they see.

Dashboards have rules the builder never mentions

Two of them decide your whole design, and you hit them after you have built five dashboards rather than before.

Running user — the default
Everyone sees the data that one person can see, whoever is looking. Right for a leadership view. Wrong for anything called "my" — every rep looking at "My Open Deals" would see the running user's deals, not their own.
Dynamic — and severely capped
Set it to run as the logged-in user and each person sees their own data. But you get a handful per org — commonly three, five on the higher editions. Not per user. Not per app. Per org.
That cap is the single most important design constraint here. You cannot give every team a personal dashboard, so stop trying. Spend your dynamic dashboards on the two or three places where seeing your own data genuinely changes behavior, and solve everything else with filters.
Filters instead of copies
A dashboard takes a small number of filters — usually three. One dashboard with a region filter replaces five regional copies, and the viewer picks their own view.
The maintenance difference is the whole argument. Five copies means five places to fix when a field changes, and four of them get forgotten. Anyone who has inherited an org has met the four.
One report, several components
A single well-grouped report can feed a chart, a table and a metric at once — each component chooses its own grouping and visual from the same source.
Build one report properly rather than three thin ones. When the definition changes you edit it once, and every component moves together instead of drifting apart.
A dashboard's reports are not reports people read
A source report is shaped for a chart: grouped, summarized, few columns, sorted for the visual. Opened directly it looks sparse and strange, because it was never meant to be opened.
Keep them in their own folder, named so it is obvious — Dashboard sources. Otherwise someone finds one, concludes reporting here is useless, and goes back to a spreadsheet.

Good report habits

An org with three hundred reports and no rules is one nobody trusts. These are the habits that keep reporting usable a year from now — and they cost nothing if you start with them.

The folder rule
Nobody saves to a public folder except an admin. Public Reports is where reporting goes to die — hundreds of half-finished attempts nobody dares delete because they might be someone's.
Everyone drafts in their private folder. When something is worth keeping, an admin moves it into a shared folder owned by a team, with access managed deliberately.
Folder access
What it allows
Who should hold it
Viewer
Run and read what is in the folder. Cannot change or add anything.
Almost everyone.
Editor
Change and save over what is there — including someone else's report, silently.
A named few, per team.
Manager
All of the above, plus deleting the folder and changing who can reach it.
Admins only.
Name for the question
"Grants closing this quarter" tells someone whether to open it. "Opportunity Report 4" makes them build their own instead.
One owner per folder
Someone has to be responsible for what is in it. A folder everyone can edit becomes a folder nobody maintains.
Prune once a year
Check the last-run date and archive anything untouched for twelve months. Ask first, then move rather than delete.
Dashboards have their own folders — and one extra trap
The same Viewer, Editor and Manager model applies, and the same rule holds: only an admin promotes a dashboard into a shared folder. People forget this because they set up report folders once and assume dashboards followed.
The trap is the running user. A dashboard shows whatever that person can see, not what the viewer can — so someone may be reading numbers drawn from records they have no access to. Set it deliberately and say out loud whose view it is.
A dashboard also needs its source reports to live somewhere the viewer can reach. Sharing the dashboard folder is not enough on its own — if the underlying report sits in your private folder, their dashboard renders empty and they will assume it is broken.
These rules feel bureaucratic until the first time someone edits the board report an hour before the meeting. Then they feel like the cheapest insurance you ever bought.

Built is not the same as used

Everything you have made exists now. That is the easy half and the half people stop at. Working means someone opened it a second time without being asked.

Four checks, in order
The number is right
It matches what the person predicted, or you both understand why it does not.
They can find it
Watch them look for it without help. Do not point. This is the most uncomfortable and most useful two minutes in the kit.
It survives their login
Their permissions and sharing are not yours. Confirm what they see, not what you see.
Someone came back
A second unprompted visit in the first fortnight. Until then you have built something, not changed anything.
Proof it worked
Write down what you replaced. "Three people were each keeping their own spreadsheet" is the sentence that justifies the next investment in your system. Report and dashboard folders have their own sharing. Access to the data does not mean access to the folder. Write the numbers down the day you launch. In three months nobody will remember what it looked like before.

When nobody uses it

This is the normal outcome, not the exceptional one. Every cause below is fixable in under an hour, and none of them is that people are lazy.

They looked once and the number seemed wrong
Why it happens
Usually a filter they did not expect, or records they cannot see. It only takes one wrong-looking number to lose someone permanently.
What to do
Sit with them and reconcile it against their spreadsheet, line by line. Then say out loud what the difference was — trust comes back through explanation, not correction.
It is not on the path they already walk
Why it happens
Three clicks is too far for something not yet a habit. Reports live somewhere people do not go.
What to do
Move it to the home page, or make it the default list view. Do not send a link — a link works once.
It answers a question nobody was asking
Why it happens
You built what was requested rather than what was wondered. The two often differ, and the requester rarely notices until they see it.
What to do
Go back to Tab 2 and ask what they would do differently. Being willing to discard a finished report is the whole skill.
Their login shows something different from yours
Why it happens
Folder sharing, record access, or a home page assigned to the wrong profile. All three are invisible from an admin account.
What to do
Check the folder first, then the home page assignment, then sharing. The security insert in this kit has the full order.
The home page never appeared at all
Why it happens
A Lightning home page is assigned per app and per profile. Assign it to the wrong app and the right people see nothing while you see it perfectly. This is the most common failure in this kit.
What to do
Ask which app they actually open in the morning — it is often not the one you assumed. Then check the assignment covers that app and their profile, and confirm from their login rather than yours.
Knowing why something was not used, and what you changed, is worth more to your organization than a dashboard nobody opens. Note which of these five it was — the pattern across a few builds tells you more about your org than any single fix.

The two weeks after you build it

Adoption is not an announcement at a staff meeting. It is a fortnight of small, slightly awkward follow-up that almost nobody does — and it is the whole difference between a system people use and one they tolerate.

Day one
Show one person, in person or on a call. Not a group, not an email. Watch them use it and say nothing while they do.
Day two
Fix whatever made them hesitate. There is always something, and it is usually a column or a name.
Day three
Now tell everyone — and say what question it answers, not what you built. Nobody has ever been excited by the word "dashboard".
Week one
Have your director use it in a meeting, out loud, in front of everyone. Nothing drives adoption in a small organization like watching leadership rely on it.
Week two
Ask the one person who has not touched it why. Their answer is worth more than everyone else's praise.
What to send on day three
If you have been wondering [the question], it is now on your home page when you log in — no report to run and no spreadsheet to update. [Name] has been using it since Monday. If the number looks wrong to you, tell me; that is useful rather than annoying.
Following up feels like nagging. It is not — it is the only way to find out whether you built the right thing, and in a small team it is the difference between a change that sticks and one that quietly reverses.
If it worked, finish the job
Make it the default
Pin the list view so it is what opens, and leave the home page in place. A habit that depends on someone choosing it every day is not yet a habit.
Retire the spreadsheet
Ask the person to stop updating it, and agree a date. Two sources of the same number is worse than one bad one, because now nobody knows which to believe.
Close the loop
Go back to whoever asked the original question and show them it is answered. People stop bringing you problems when they never hear what happened to the last one.
Then write down the date and what changed. In six months somebody will ask whether this was worth doing, and a sentence written today answers that far better than a memory will.
